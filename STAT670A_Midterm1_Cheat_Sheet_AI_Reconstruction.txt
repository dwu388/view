# 1 Set Theory and Events

## Core objects

- **Experiment**: a process with an uncertain outcome.

- **Sample space** $S$: set of all possible outcomes. It may be discrete (finite or countably infinite) or continuous (uncountable).

- **Event** $A$: any subset of $S$. The event occurs when the observed outcome lies in $A$.

- **Empty event** $\varnothing$: impossible event. **Certain event**: $S$.

## Operations

|                  |                                                      |
|:-----------------|:-----------------------------------------------------|
| **Union**        | $A\cup B$: $A$ or $B$ (or both) occurs.              |
| **Intersection** | $A\cap B$: both $A$ and $B$ occur.                   |
| **Complement**   | $A^c$: $A$ does not occur.                           |
| **Difference**   | $A\setminus B=A\cap B^c$.                            |
| **Subset**       | $A\subseteq B$: every outcome in $A$ is also in $B$. |

## Event algebra

$$\begin{gathered}
A\cup B=B\cup A,\qquad A\cap B=B\cap A,\\
A\cup(B\cup C)=(A\cup B)\cup C,\\
A\cap(B\cap C)=(A\cap B)\cap C,\\
A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C),\\
A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C),\\
(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c,\qquad
(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c.
\end{gathered}$$

**Disjoint (mutually exclusive):** $A\cap B=\varnothing$. Events $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ are pairwise disjoint if $A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing$ for every $i\ne j$.

**Partition of $S$:** a collection $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ that is pairwise disjoint and satisfies $\bigcup_i A_i=S$. Exactly one partition cell contains each outcome.

# 2 Probability Foundations

## Sigma algebra

A collection $\mathcal B$ of subsets of $S$ is a sigma algebra when:

1.  $\varnothing\in\mathcal B$;

2.  $A\in\mathcal B\Rightarrow A^c\in\mathcal B$;

3.  $A_1,A_2,\ldots\in\mathcal B\Rightarrow \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_i\in\mathcal B$.

These closure rules identify the events to which probabilities may be assigned.

## Probability axioms

For $A\in\mathcal B$:

1.  $P(A)\ge 0$;

2.  $P(S)=1$;

3.  if $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ are pairwise disjoint, then $$P\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)=\sum_iP(A_i).$$

For a finite or countable sample space with point masses $p_i\ge0$ and $\sum_i p_i=1$, define $P(A)=\sum_{i:s_i\in A}p_i$.

## Essential consequences

$$\begin{aligned}
P(\varnothing)&=0, & 0\le P(A)&\le1,\\
P(A^c)&=1-P(A),\\
P(B\cap A^c)&=P(B)-P(A\cap B),\\
P(A\cup B)&=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B),\\
A\subseteq B&\Rightarrow P(A)\le P(B).
\end{aligned}$$

**Bonferroni lower bound:** $$P(A\cap B)\ge P(A)+P(B)-1.$$ **Boole union bound:** $$P\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)\le\sum_iP(A_i).$$ If $C_1,C_2,\ldots$ partitions $S$, then $$P(A)=\sum_iP(A\cap C_i)=\sum_iP(A\mid C_i)P(C_i).$$

## Useful proof pattern

If $P(A\cap B)=P(A)+P(B)$, inclusion-exclusion gives $$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)=0.$$ Since $A,B\subseteq A\cup B$, monotonicity yields $P(A)=P(B)=0$.

# 3 Counting and Equally Likely Outcomes

**Multiplication rule:** if task $i$ can be completed in $n_i$ ways, a sequence of $k$ tasks has $\prod_{i=1}^k n_i$ outcomes.

$$n!=n(n-1)\cdots2\cdot1,\qquad 0!=1,
\qquad \binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}.$$

|           | Without replacement  | With replacement   |
|:----------|:---------------------|:-------------------|
| Ordered   | $\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ | $n^r$              |
| Unordered | $\binom nr$          | $\binom{n+r-1}{r}$ |

When all $N$ outcomes are equally likely, $$P(A)=\frac{|A|}{|S|}.$$

## Counting decision checklist

1.  Does order matter?

2.  Is replacement allowed?

3.  Are repeated objects distinguishable?

4.  Count the entire sample space and the favorable outcomes under the *same* convention.

## Five-card examples

All hands are unordered and without replacement, so $|S|=\binom{52}{5}$.

- Four aces: choose the fifth card, so $|A|=\binom{48}{1}$.

- Four of a kind: choose the rank and side card, so $|A|=\binom{13}{1}\binom{48}{1}$.

- Exactly one pair: choose pair rank, 2 suits, 3 other ranks, and one suit for each other rank: $$|A|=\binom{13}{1}\binom{4}{2}\binom{12}{3}4^3.$$

# 4 Conditioning Bayes and Independence

## Conditional probability and multiplication

For $P(B)>0$, $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad
P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B).$$ Also $P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)$, and conditional probabilities obey the ordinary probability axioms within the restricted universe $B$.

## Law of total probability

If $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ partitions $S$, $$P(B)=\sum_jP(B\mid A_j)P(A_j).$$ Use it when an outcome can occur through several mutually exclusive routes.

## Bayes rule

$$P(A_i\mid B)=
\frac{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}{\sum_jP(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}.$$ Interpretation: posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior. The denominator is the total probability of the observed evidence.

## Two-stage selection template

If the first-stage result changes the second-stage composition, condition on the first-stage result: $$P(\text{target at stage 2})
=\sum_jP(\text{target at stage 2}\mid C_j)P(C_j).$$ For the applicant-list problem: $$P(\text{man at stage 2})=\frac35\frac25+\frac25\frac35=\frac{12}{25}.$$

## Independence

$A$ and $B$ are independent exactly when $$P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).$$ When the relevant conditional probability exists, this is equivalent to $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ or $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$.

If $A\perp\!\!\!\perp B$, then $A\perp\!\!\!\perp B^c$, $A^c\perp\!\!\!\perp B$, and $A^c\perp\!\!\!\perp B^c$.

**Mutual independence:** every subcollection must satisfy $$P\!\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^k A_{i_j}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^kP(A_{i_j}).$$ Pairwise independence alone does not guarantee mutual independence. Likewise, one equality involving a triple intersection does not establish pairwise independence.

# 5 Random Variables CDFs PMFs and PDFs

## Random variable

A random variable $X$ is a function $X:S\to\mathbb{R}$. Uppercase denotes the random variable; lowercase $x$ denotes a realized value. Its support is the set of values it can take with positive mass or density.

For a discrete $X$, $$P_X(X=x_i)=P\{s\in S:X(s)=x_i\}.$$

## CDF

$$F_X(x)=P(X\le x).$$ A function is a CDF exactly when it is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and has limits $0$ at $-\infty$ and $1$ at $+\infty$.

- A discrete CDF is a step function; a continuous random variable has a continuous CDF.

- A CDF need not be strictly increasing.

- $X$ and $Y$ are identically distributed iff $F_X(x)=F_Y(x)$ for every $x$.

- Equal means and variances do *not* imply identical distributions.

## PMF and PDF rules

For a discrete $X$, $f_X(x)=P(X=x)$ and $\sum_x f_X(x)=1$.

For a continuous $X$, $$F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt,
\qquad
f_X(x)=F_X'(x)$$ where the derivative exists. A valid pmf/pdf is nonnegative and sums/integrates to 1.

For continuous $X$, $P(X=x)=0$, so endpoints do not change an interval probability: $$P(a<X<b)=P(a\le X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).$$ A density value $f_X(x)$ is not itself a point probability.

## Finding an unknown normalizing constant

If $f_X(x)=c\,h(x)$ on support $\mathcal X$, solve $$1=\int_{\mathcal X}c\,h(x)\,dx
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
c=\left(\int_{\mathcal X}h(x)\,dx\right)^{-1},$$ after checking $h(x)\ge0$.

# 6 Transformations of Random Variables

Let $Y=g(X)$. Determine the support first: $$\mathcal X=\{x:f_X(x)>0\},\qquad
\mathcal Y=\{g(x):x\in\mathcal X\}.$$

## Universal CDF method

$$F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)
=\int_{\{x\in\mathcal X:g(x)\le y\}}f_X(x)\,dx.$$ This works even when $g$ is not one-to-one. Carefully solve the inequality and split the preimage into all relevant intervals.

## Discrete transformation

$$f_Y(y)=\sum_{x:g(x)=y}f_X(x),\qquad y\in\mathcal Y.$$ Add the masses of *all* $x$ values mapping to the same $y$.

## Continuous monotone transformation

If $g$ is one-to-one and monotone, $$f_Y(y)=f_X\!\left(g^{-1}(y)\right)
\left|\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|,\qquad y\in\mathcal Y.$$ For the CDF: $$F_Y(y)=
\begin{cases}
F_X(g^{-1}(y)), & g\text{ increasing},\\
1-F_X(g^{-1}(y)), & g\text{ decreasing and }X\text{ continuous}.
\end{cases}$$

## Piecewise monotone many-to-one transformation

Partition $\mathcal X$ into monotone branches $A_i$. If $g_i$ is the restriction on branch $i$, $$f_Y(y)=\sum_i f_X\!\left(g_i^{-1}(y)\right)
\left|\frac{d}{dy}g_i^{-1}(y)\right|.$$ For $Y=X^2$, both $x=\sqrt y$ and $x=-\sqrt y$ contribute whenever both lie in the support.

## Probability integral transform

If $F_X$ is continuous, $$U=F_X(X)\sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).$$ This is the central reason a transformed continuous CDF value is uniform.

## Worked transformation patterns

- For $Y=\sqrt X$: $x=y^2$, $|dx/dy|=2y$, and transform the support before substituting.

- For $Z=1-e^{-X/\beta}$ when that expression is the CDF of $X$: $Z=F_X(X)$, so the probability integral transform gives Uniform$(0,1)$ immediately.

- For $Y=-\log X$ with $0<X<1$: the map is decreasing, $x=e^{-y}$, and $y>0$.

- For $Y=1/X$ with $X>0$: $x=1/y$, $|dx/dy|=1/y^2$, and $y>0$.

# 7 Expectation Moments and Variance

## Expectation of a function

$$\operatorname{E}[g(X)]=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal X}g(x)f_X(x), & X\text{ discrete},\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal X}g(x)f_X(x)\,dx, & X\text{ continuous}.
\end{cases}$$ The expectation exists when $\operatorname{E}|g(X)|<\infty$. A Cauchy random variable is the standard counterexample: its mean does not exist.

## Expectation properties

Whenever the expectations exist: $$\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{E}[ag_1(X)+bg_2(X)+c]
&=a\operatorname{E}[g_1(X)]+b\operatorname{E}[g_2(X)]+c,\\
g_1(x)\ge0&\Rightarrow \operatorname{E}[g_1(X)]\ge0,\\
g_1(x)\le g_2(x)&\Rightarrow \operatorname{E}[g_1(X)]\le\operatorname{E}[g_2(X)],\\
a\le g(X)\le b&\Rightarrow a\le\operatorname{E}[g(X)]\le b.
\end{aligned}$$

## Moments

The $n$th raw moment and $n$th central moment are $$\mu_n'=\operatorname{E}(X^n),\qquad \mu_n=\operatorname{E}[(X-\mu)^n],\quad \mu=\operatorname{E} X.$$ Variance is the second central moment: $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{E}[(X-\operatorname{E} X)^2]=\operatorname{E}(X^2)-[\operatorname{E} X]^2.$$ Standard deviation is $\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$.

$$\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X),\qquad
\operatorname{SD}(aX+b)=|a|\operatorname{SD}(X).$$

**Nonnegativity check:** $\operatorname{Var}(X)\ge0$, hence $\operatorname{E}(X^2)\ge[\operatorname{E} X]^2$.

## Gamma-function moment identity used in the practice set

For the course’s shape-scale parameterization and positive integers $n$ and $\alpha$, $$\operatorname{E}(X^n)=\beta^n\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
=\beta^n\frac{(\alpha+n-1)!}{(\alpha-1)!}.$$ Key identities: $$\Gamma(a+1)=a\Gamma(a),\qquad \Gamma(m)=(m-1)!\ \text{for integer }m\ge1.$$

# 8 Moment Generating Functions

## Definition and existence

$$M_X(t)=\operatorname{E}(e^{tX}).$$ An MGF exists only if the expectation is finite throughout some open neighborhood of $t=0$. For discrete and continuous variables, compute it with the usual expectation sum or integral.

## Recovering moments

$$M_X^{(n)}(0)=\operatorname{E}(X^n).$$ Thus $$\operatorname{E} X=M_X'(0),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=M_X''(0)-[M_X'(0)]^2.$$

## Linear transformation

$$M_{aX+b}(t)=e^{bt}M_X(at).$$

## Identification and convergence

- If MGFs exist and agree on a neighborhood of 0, the corresponding CDFs are identical.

- For bounded-support variables, equality of all moments is equivalent to equality of the distributions.

- If $M_{X_n}(t)\to M_X(t)$ near 0 and the limit is an MGF, then $F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)$ at continuity points of $F_X$.

## MGF workflow

1.  Write $\operatorname{E}(e^{tX})$ as a sum or integral.

2.  Combine $e^{tx}$ with the kernel already containing $x$.

3.  Recognize a normalized pmf/pdf or use a known algebraic expansion.

4.  State the allowable values of $t$.

5.  Differentiate only after simplifying.

# 9 Approximation Results

## Poisson approximation to a binomial count

When $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $np=\lambda$ is moderate, $$\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)\approx\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda).$$ The lecture derives this by MGF convergence. This approximation is useful for rare events across many independent opportunities.

## Central limit theorem

For i.i.d. $X_i$ with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, $$\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\ \approx\ N(n\mu,n\sigma^2)
\quad\text{for large }n.$$ Equivalently, $$\frac{\sum_iX_i-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\approx N(0,1).$$ For a discrete count approximated by a continuous normal variable, use a continuity correction: $$P(X\le k)\approx P(Y<k+0.5),\qquad
P(X\ge k)\approx P(Y>k-0.5).$$

# 10 Distribution Recognition Guide

Identify a distribution from the structure of the experiment, not from a single keyword. First decide whether the random variable is discrete or continuous. Then ask what is fixed, what is counted or measured, whether sampling stops at a target success, and whether replacement occurs.

## Discrete distributions at a glance

| Distribution | Keywords or structural clues | Main question |
|:-------------|:-----------------------------|:--------------|
| **Bernoulli** | one trial, success/failure, yes/no, defective/not defective | Is there exactly **one binary trial**? |
| **Binomial** | fixed number $n$, number of successes, independent trials, constant $p$ | Am I counting successes in a **fixed number of trials**? |
| **Geometric** | until first success, first defective, first arrival | How many trials until the **first success**? |
| **Negative binomial** | until the $r$th success, second defective, third success | How many trials or failures until the **$r$th success**? |
| **Poisson** | number of events, arrivals, defects, calls, accidents, per hour/day/area | Am I counting events in a **fixed interval** at rate $\lambda$? |
| **Hypergeometric** | without replacement, finite population, sample from $N$, exactly $M$ successes in the population | Am I sampling **without replacement**? |

## Bernoulli

Look for one item or one trial with only two possible outcomes, such as defective/not defective or success/failure.

Example: a randomly selected unit has a 10% probability of being defective. Define

$$
X=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{defective},\\
0, & \text{not defective}.
\end{cases}
$$

Then

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.1).
$$

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{one yes/no trial}}
$$

## Binomial

Look for $n$ trials, a count of successes, independence, and the same success probability $p$ on every trial.

Example: Manager A selects 10 units and counts how many are defective. Then

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,0.1).
$$

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{fixed }n+\text{count successes}}
$$

The trials should be independent with constant $p$.

## Geometric

Look for “until the first success,” “first defective,” “first failure,” or “number of attempts before the first success.”

Example: inspect units until the first defective is found. Then

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Geometric}(p).
$$

Under the course convention, $X=1,2,3,\ldots$ counts the trial on which the first success occurs.

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{continue until the first success}}
$$

The geometric distribution is the negative binomial special case $r=1$.

## Negative binomial

Look for “until the second success,” “until the third defective,” “the $r$th success,” or “keep sampling until $r$ successes.”

Example: Manager B checks units until he finds the second defective. The stopping rule is reaching success number $r=2$, so a negative binomial model applies.

Two common variable definitions are:

- **Number of trials until the $r$th success:** $x=r,r+1,\ldots$
- **Number of failures before the $r$th success:** $y=0,1,2,\ldots$

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{continue until success number }r}
$$

Always state which convention is being used before applying a formula.

## Poisson

Look for a count of occurrences in a fixed amount of time, space, area, length, or volume, together with a rate $\lambda$.

Examples include customers arriving in one hour, defects in one square meter, or typing errors on a page. Typically,

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda),
$$

with

$$
\operatorname{E}(X)=\lambda,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda.
$$

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{count events in a fixed time or space interval}}
$$

If the rate is stated per unit, rescale it to the full interval before calculating.

## Hypergeometric

The decisive clue is

$$
\boxed{\text{sampling without replacement}}.
$$

Look for a finite population of $N$ objects, $M$ objects with the characteristic of interest, and a sample of $K$ objects selected without replacement.

Example: a box contains 20 components, 5 defective. Select 4 without replacement and count the number of defectives. This is hypergeometric.

Key comparison:

$$
\boxed{\text{Binomial: independent trials or replacement}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Hypergeometric: sampling without replacement}}
$$

## Continuous distributions at a glance

| Distribution | Keywords or structural clues |
|:-------------|:-----------------------------|
| **Uniform** | equally likely anywhere between $a$ and $b$, constant density |
| **Normal** | bell-shaped, symmetric, mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$ |
| **Exponential** | waiting time until one event, lifetime, time between Poisson events |
| **Gamma** | waiting time until several events, total waiting time, positive continuous variable |
| **Chi-square** | variance inference, sum of squared standard normals, degrees of freedom |
| **Beta** | probability, proportion, or fraction between 0 and 1 |

## Uniform

Look for “equally likely between $a$ and $b$,” a random point in an interval, or a constant density. Its support is

$$
a\le X\le b.
$$

Example: a bus arrives at a random time between 2:00 and 2:20, with every arrival time equally likely.

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{equally likely over an interval}}
$$

## Normal

Look for “normal,” “Gaussian,” “bell-shaped,” “symmetric,” or “approximately normal.” The standard parameterization is

$$
X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2).
$$

Typical examples are heights, test scores, measurement errors, or quantities explicitly stated to be normally distributed.

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{continuous and symmetric bell-shaped distribution}}
$$

## Exponential

Look for waiting time until the next event, time until failure, lifetime, or time between arrivals.

Under the course scale parameterization,

$$
\operatorname{E}(X)=\beta.
$$

Example: if calls follow a Poisson process, the waiting time until the next call is exponential.

Useful connection:

$$
\boxed{\text{Poisson: number of events}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Exponential: waiting time until the next event}}
$$

## Gamma

Look for waiting time until several events, total waiting time, or another positive continuous measurement. Under the course parameterization,

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta),
\qquad
\operatorname{E}(X)=\alpha\beta.
$$

Conceptually, exponential models waiting until the first event, while gamma can model waiting until several events. For example, “How long until the fifth arrival?” suggests a gamma model.

## Chi-square

Look for $\chi^2$, degrees of freedom, sample variance, inference about a variance, or a sum of squared standard normal variables.

Under the course notation,

$$
X\sim\chi^2(p),
\qquad
\operatorname{E}(X)=p,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=2p.
$$

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{variance and degrees of freedom}}
$$

This distribution appears especially often in statistical inference.

## Beta

Look for a continuous probability, proportion, or fraction restricted to

$$
0\le X\le1.
$$

Recognition pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{continuous proportion between 0 and 1}}
$$

## Fastest exam decision tree

For a discrete random variable, first ask whether the experiment consists of success/failure trials.

- One trial: **Bernoulli**
- Fixed $n$, count successes: **Binomial**
- Continue until first success: **Geometric**
- Continue until the $r$th success: **Negative binomial**
- Sample without replacement from a finite population: **Hypergeometric**
- Count events over a fixed time or space interval: **Poisson**

For a continuous random variable:

- Equally likely over $[a,b]$: **Uniform**
- Symmetric bell shape: **Normal**
- Waiting until the next event: **Exponential**
- Waiting until several events: **Gamma**
- Variance or degrees of freedom: **Chi-square**
- Proportion between 0 and 1: **Beta**

The three most important distinctions are:

$$
\boxed{\text{Binomial: fixed trials, count successes}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Negative binomial: fixed successes, count trials or failures}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Poisson: fixed interval, count events}}
$$

# 11 Distribution Material Not on the Exclusion Sheet

## Discrete uniform on $\{1,\ldots,N\}$

$$P(X=x\mid N)=\frac1N,\quad x=1,\ldots,N,$$ $$\operatorname{E} X=\frac{N+1}{2},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{(N+1)(N-1)}{12}.$$

## Lognormal

If $\log X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then $X$ is lognormal and $X=e^Y$ for $Y\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Its density, obtained by transformation, is $$f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma x}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\log x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\qquad x>0.$$

# 12 Exponential Families

## General form

A family is exponential if its pmf/pdf can be written $$f(x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=h(x)c(\boldsymbol\theta)
\exp\!\left\{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i(\boldsymbol\theta)t_i(x)\right\}.$$ Separation is essential:

- $h(x)$ and every $t_i(x)$ may depend on $x$ but not on $\boldsymbol\theta$.

- $c(\boldsymbol\theta)$ and every $w_i(\boldsymbol\theta)$ may depend on parameters but not on $x$.

- The support indicator belongs in $h(x)$ and must not depend on the unknown parameter.

The indicator of a set $A$ is $$\mathbf{1}_A(x)=
\begin{cases}
1,&x\in A,\\
0,&x\notin A.
\end{cases}$$

## Recognition workflow

1.  Write powers as exponentials: $a^b=\exp(b\log a)$.

2.  Expand squares or products that mix $x$ and parameters.

3.  Group $x$-only, parameter-only, and separable product terms.

4.  Express support with an indicator.

5.  If the support depends on an unknown parameter, the stated family generally fails this fixed-support exponential-family form.

The representation is not unique; constants can be redistributed among $h$, $c$, $w_i$, and $t_i$ while preserving separation.

## Why some candidates fail

- In the Weibull family with both shape $\gamma$ and scale $\beta$ unknown, the term $x^\gamma$ cannot be separated into $w(\gamma,\beta)t(x)$ with a fixed finite set of $x$-statistics. Hence the two-parameter family is not exponential in this form.

- For $f(x\mid\theta)=\theta^{-1}\exp(1-x/\theta)\mathbf{1}_{(\theta,\infty)}(x)$, the support indicator depends on both $x$ and $\theta$, so it cannot serve as $h(x)$.

## Lognormal example

Expand $$-\frac{(\log x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
=-\frac{(\log x)^2}{2\sigma^2}
+\frac{\mu\log x}{\sigma^2}
-\frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$ This separates using statistics $t_1(x)=-(\log x)^2/2$ and $t_2(x)=\log x$, so the lognormal family is exponential.

## Natural parameterization

Write $$f(x\mid\boldsymbol\eta)=h(x)c^*(\boldsymbol\eta)
\exp\!\left\{\sum_{i=1}^k\eta_i t_i(x)\right\}.$$ The natural parameter space is $$\mathcal H=\left\{\boldsymbol\eta:
\int h(x)\exp\!\left(\sum_i\eta_it_i(x)\right)dx<\infty\right\}.$$ For a normal family, one common choice is $\eta_1=\mu/\sigma^2$ and $\eta_2=-1/(2\sigma^2)$, so $\eta_2<0$. For a binomial family with fixed $n$, $\eta=\log[p/(1-p)]\in\mathbb{R}$.

## Full versus curved

If the original parameter vector has dimension $d$ and the natural statistic vector has dimension $k$, the family is **full** when $d=k$ and **curved** when $d<k$.

# 13 Location and Scale Families

Given a standard density $f$:

- **Location family**: $g(x\mid\mu)=f(x-\mu)$.

- **Scale family**: $g(x\mid\sigma)=\sigma^{-1}f(x/\sigma)$, $\sigma>0$.

- **Location-scale family**: $$g(x\mid\mu,\sigma)=\frac1\sigma
    f\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right),\qquad\sigma>0.$$

The $1/\sigma$ factor is the Jacobian that makes the density integrate to 1.

Equivalently, if $Z$ has standard density $f$, then $$X=\sigma Z+\mu.$$ When the moments exist, $$\operatorname{E} X=\sigma\operatorname{E} Z+\mu,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2\operatorname{Var}(Z).$$ For probabilities, $$P(X\le x)=P\!\left(Z\le\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).$$

# 14 Probability Inequalities

## General nonnegative-function bound

For $g(X)\ge0$ and $r>0$, $$P(g(X)\ge r)\le\frac{\operatorname{E}[g(X)]}{r}.$$ This is the Markov/Chebyshev template in the lecture notes.

Set $g(X)=(X-\mu)^2$ and $r=k^2\sigma^2$ to obtain $$P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le\frac1{k^2},$$ or equivalently $$P(|X-\mu|<k\sigma)\ge1-\frac1{k^2}.$$

## Vysochanskii-Petunin inequality

For a unimodal density, let $\xi^2=\operatorname{E}[(X-\alpha)^2]$. Then $$P(|X-\alpha|>\epsilon)\le
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{4\xi^2}{9\epsilon^2}, & \epsilon\ge\sqrt{8/3}\,\xi,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{4\xi^2}{3\epsilon^2}-\dfrac13, & \epsilon\le\sqrt{8/3}\,\xi.
\end{cases}$$ With $\alpha=\mu$ and $\epsilon=3\sigma$, the first branch gives $$P(|X-\mu|>3\sigma)\le\frac4{81},$$ a stronger bound than Chebyshev’s $1/9$ for unimodal distributions.

# 15 Worked Problem Recognition and Exam Traps

## Define the random variable before calculating

State exactly what is counted or measured, its support, and the relevant assumptions. Examples from the practice set:

- “Number of defectives among 10 independent units” is a fixed-number-of-trials count.

- “Trial on which the second defective occurs” is a waiting-time-to-the-$r$th-success count.

- “Number of imperfections in 3 square yards” rescales the rate from 2 per square yard to 6 for the full area.

## Complement strategy

For “at least one,” calculate $$P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0).$$ For “at least two,” calculate $$P(X\ge2)=1-P(X=0)-P(X=1).$$ This is usually shorter and less error-prone than summing the upper tail.

For the practice manufacturing problem, if $X$ counts defectives among 10 units, $$P(\text{Manager A stops})
=1-(0.9)^{10}-10(0.1)(0.9)^9.$$ If $Y$ is the trial on which the second defective appears, $$P(\text{Manager B stops})
=\sum_{y=2}^{10}(y-1)(0.1)^2(0.9)^{y-2}.$$ For the textile problem, a rate of 2 per square yard becomes 6 over 3 square yards, so $$P(\text{at least one imperfection})=1-e^{-6}.$$

## Support conventions matter

For a waiting-time variable, decide whether it counts total trials or failures before the target success. The lower endpoint changes, so always write the random-variable definition and support before using a formula.

## True or false lessons

- Same mean and variance $\not\Rightarrow$ same distribution; same CDF $\Leftrightarrow$ same distribution.

- If $P(B)>0$ and $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$, then $A$ and $B$ are independent.

- A CDF is nondecreasing, not necessarily strictly increasing.

- $\operatorname{E}|X|$ need not exist for every random variable.

- The gamma density is asymmetric for its allowable parameter values.

## Final answer checklist

1.  Define events or random variables.

2.  State support and parameterization.

3.  Name the rule or theorem being used.

4.  Show the substitution or probability decomposition.

5.  Check that the result is between 0 and 1 and matches the event’s direction.

# 16 Compact Algebra and Calculus Identities

$$\begin{aligned}
\log(e^a)&=a, & e^{\log x}&=x\quad(x>0),\\
\log(ab)&=\log a+\log b, &
\log(a^b)&=b\log a,\\
\frac{d}{dx}\log x&=\frac1x, &
\frac{d}{dx}e^{g(x)}&=e^{g(x)}g'(x),\\
\frac{d}{dx}(1-x)^{b}&=-b(1-x)^{b-1},\\
\frac{d}{dx}\log(1-x)&=-\frac1{1-x}.
\end{aligned}$$

**Binomial theorem:** $$(a+b)^n=\sum_{i=0}^n\binom ni a^ib^{n-i}.$$

**Change-of-variable reminder:** after finding $x=g^{-1}(y)$, use the absolute Jacobian $|dx/dy|$ and the transformed support. Never stop after substituting into $f_X$.

**Exponential-family reminder:** terms involving both $x$ and an unknown parameter are allowed only when they separate into a parameter-only coefficient times an $x$-only statistic. A parameter-dependent support is the major red flag.

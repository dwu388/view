1. REGRESSION GOAL, DATA, AND NOTATION

Regression models the relationship between predictor(s) and a response.
Estimation describes the population mean relationship using observed data;
prediction forecasts an outcome at specified input values. Simple linear
regression has one predictor $x$ (any type) and one continuous response $Y$.

Data types:
- Numeric: discrete counts or continuous measurements.
- Categorical: nominal labels or ordinal levels.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x_i, Y_i$ | Observed predictor and response for experimental unit $i$. |
| $\hat{Y}_i$ | Fitted response from the sample regression line. |
| $e_i$ | Residual: $e_i=Y_i-\hat{Y}_i$; observable surrogate for $\varepsilon_i$. |
| $\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2$ | Population intercept, slope, and common error variance. |
| $b_0,b_1,\mathrm{MSE}$ | Sample estimates of $\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2$. |


2. POPULATION MODEL AND LINE CONDITIONS

Population model and conditional mean:

$$
Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
E(Y_i)=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i.
$$

Sample fitted line and residual:

$$
\hat{Y}_i=b_0+b_1x_i,
\qquad
e_i=Y_i-\hat{Y}_i.
$$

The four LINE conditions:
- L - Linear mean: $E(Y_i)$ is linear in $x_i$.
- I - Independent errors: $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$ are independent.
- N - Normal errors: errors at each $x$ are normally distributed.
- E - Equal variance: $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2$ for every $x$.

Compactly:

$$
\varepsilon_i\text{ are independent }N(0,\sigma^2).
$$

NOTE - Normality:
The assumption concerns errors conditional on predictor values, not
necessarily the marginal distributions of $x$ or $Y$.


3. LEAST-SQUARES ESTIMATION

Choose $b_0$ and $b_1$ to minimize the sum of squared residuals:

$$
Q=\sum_{i=1}^n\left[Y_i-(b_0+b_1x_i)\right]^2
  =\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2
  =\mathrm{SSE}.
$$

Normal equations from differentiation:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0,
$$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(Y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0.
$$

Computational sums:

$$
S_{xx}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})^2
      =\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2-n\bar{x}^2,
$$

$$
S_{yy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2
      =\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^2-n\bar{Y}^2,
$$

$$
S_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})(Y_i-\bar{Y})
      =\sum_{i=1}^n x_iY_i-n\bar{x}\bar{Y}.
$$

Least-squares estimates:

$$
b_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},
\qquad
b_0=\bar{Y}-b_1\bar{x}.
$$

Important properties:
- The fitted line passes through $(\bar{x},\bar{Y})$.
- $\sum e_i=0$.
- $\sum x_ie_i=0$.
- The average fitted response equals the average observed response:
  $\overline{\hat{Y}}=\bar{Y}$.
- $S_{xx}>0$, so $\operatorname{sign}(b_1)=\operatorname{sign}(S_{xy})$.


4. COEFFICIENT INTERPRETATION

| Estimate | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| $b_0$ | Predicted response when $x=0$. Interpret only if zero is meaningful and within a useful range. |
| $b_1$ | Estimated change in the mean response per one-unit increase in $x$. Units are response units per predictor unit. |

WARNING - Extrapolation:
Predictions and intercept interpretations outside the observed $x$ range may
be meaningless. In the height-weight example, the negative intercept
corresponds to an impossible height of zero.


5. PEARSON CORRELATION

$$
r_{xy}=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}},
\qquad
-1\le r_{xy}\le 1.
$$

- $r$ is a unitless measure of the direction and strength of a linear
  association.
- $r=1$ or $r=-1$ indicates a perfect positive or negative line.
- $r=0$ indicates no linear association.
- Slope-correlation relationships:

$$
b_1=r\sqrt{\frac{S_{yy}}{S_{xx}}},
\qquad
r=b_1\sqrt{\frac{S_{xx}}{S_{yy}}}.
$$

Therefore, the slope and correlation have the same sign.


6. VARIATION DECOMPOSITION AND R-SQUARED

Observation-level decomposition:

$$
Y_i-\bar{Y}=(Y_i-\hat{Y}_i)+(\hat{Y}_i-\bar{Y}).
$$

| Quantity | Definition and meaning |
|---|---|
| $\mathrm{SSTO}$ | $\sum(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2$: total response variation. |
| $\mathrm{SSR}$ | $\sum(\hat{Y}_i-\bar{Y})^2$: variation explained by regression. |
| $\mathrm{SSE}$ | $\sum(Y_i-\hat{Y}_i)^2$: unexplained or error variation. |

$$
\mathrm{SSTO}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE}.
$$

$$
R^2=\frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SSTO}}
   =1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SSTO}}.
$$

- $0\le R^2\le 1$.
- Interpret $100R^2\%$ as the percentage of variation in $Y$ explained,
  or reduced, by accounting for $x$ through the fitted line.
- In simple regression with an intercept, $R^2=r^2$.
- Recover the sign of $r$ from the fitted slope.

Examples:

| Relationship | Summary |
|---|---|
| Height vs GPA | $R^2=0.0028$, $r=-0.053$: almost no linear association. |
| Mortality vs latitude | $R^2=0.6798$, $r\approx-0.82$: fairly strong negative linear association. |
| Fahrenheit vs Celsius | $R^2=1$, $r=1$: perfect positive line. |
| Stories vs height | $r=0.951$, $R^2=90.4\%$. |
| Age vs seeing distance | $r=-0.801$, $R^2=64.2\%$. |


7. ESTIMATING ERROR VARIANCE

Without a predictor:

$$
s^2=\frac{\sum(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2}{n-1}.
$$

With a fitted line, residuals replace deviations from $\bar{Y}$:

$$
\mathrm{MSE}
=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n-2}
=\frac{\sum(Y_i-\hat{Y}_i)^2}{n-2}.
$$

$$
\hat{\sigma}^2=\mathrm{MSE},
\qquad
\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}},
\qquad
E(\mathrm{MSE})=\sigma^2.
$$

- The denominator is $n-2$ because $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are estimated.
- Smaller MSE means responses cluster more tightly around the fitted line and
  inference is more precise, all else equal.


8. STANDARD ERRORS AND SAMPLING DISTRIBUTIONS

$$
\operatorname{se}(b_1)=\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}.
$$

$$
\operatorname{se}(b_0)
=\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}}.
$$

When the LINE conditions hold:

$$
\frac{b_1-\beta_1}{\operatorname{se}(b_1)}\sim t_{n-2},
$$

$$
\frac{b_0-\beta_0}{\operatorname{se}(b_0)}\sim t_{n-2}.
$$

Lower MSE, larger predictor spread $S_{xx}$, and generally larger $n$ reduce
standard errors.


9. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS FOR COEFFICIENTS

Slope:

$$
b_1\pm t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(b_1).
$$

Intercept:

$$
b_0\pm t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(b_0).
$$

A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ interval has the form

$$
\text{sample estimate}\pm
(\text{$t$ multiplier})(\text{standard error}).
$$

Interpret the slope interval in response units per predictor unit. Interval
width is

$$
2t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(b_1).
$$

It increases with confidence level and MSE, and decreases with $S_{xx}$ and
sample information.

NOTE - Confidence meaning:
The confidence level is the long-run coverage of the interval procedure.
After sampling, both the parameter and computed interval are fixed.


10. GOODNESS-OF-FIT CAUTIONS AND STUDY DESIGN

- A large $R^2$ does not prove that a straight line fits well. A curved
  pattern may still have high $R^2$.
- One or a few influential observations can greatly change the slope, $r$,
  and $R^2$.
- Association is not causation. In an observational study, predictor values
  are merely observed and confounding may explain the association.
- In a well-designed experiment, the researcher controls predictor values;
  causal conclusions may be supported when the design is valid.


11. HYPOTHESIS-TEST LOGIC

- State $H_0$ and $H_1$ about a population parameter.
- Choose $\alpha$, often 0.05.
- Calculate a statistic whose null distribution is known.
- Find the p-value or equivalent rejection region.
- The p-value is the probability, assuming $H_0$, of data at least as extreme
  as observed.
- Reject $H_0$ if $p<\alpha$; otherwise fail to reject $H_0$.
- Never write "accept $H_0$."


12. T TESTS FOR SLOPE AND INTERCEPT

Two-sided slope test:

$$
H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10}
\qquad\text{vs}\qquad
H_1:\beta_1\ne\beta_{10}.
$$

$$
t^*=\frac{b_1-\beta_{10}}{\operatorname{se}(b_1)}
\sim t_{n-2}\quad\text{under }H_0.
$$

$$
p=2P\left(t_{n-2}>|t^*|\right).
$$

Reject when

$$
|t^*|>t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}.
$$

The usual regression test $H_0:\beta_1=0$ asks whether there is evidence of
a population linear association. For this test:

$$
t^*=\frac{b_1}{\operatorname{se}(b_1)}.
$$

Two-sided intercept test:

$$
H_0:\beta_0=\beta_{00}
\qquad\text{vs}\qquad
H_1:\beta_0\ne\beta_{00}.
$$

$$
t^*=\frac{b_0-\beta_{00}}{\operatorname{se}(b_0)}
\sim t_{n-2}\quad\text{under }H_0.
$$

For the same $\alpha$, a two-sided test rejects exactly when the corresponding
$100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval excludes the null value.

NOTE - One-sided tests:
For a prespecified $\beta<$ value or $\beta>$ value alternative, use the signed
$t$ statistic and the appropriate one-tail probability. Do not select the
direction after viewing the data.


13. MEAN RESPONSE VS ONE NEW RESPONSE

| Target | Wording and parameter |
|---|---|
| Mean response | Average, expected, or typical response for all units at $x_h$; target $E(Y_h)$. |
| New response | One individual, a particular future unit, or tomorrow's value; target $Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}$. |

Common point estimate:

$$
\hat{Y}_h=b_0+b_1x_h.
$$

Confidence interval for the mean response $E(Y_h)$:

$$
\operatorname{se}(\hat{Y}_h)
=\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{
\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(x_h-\bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}
}.
$$

$$
\hat{Y}_h\pm
t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(\hat{Y}_h).
$$

Prediction interval for one new response $Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}$:

$$
\operatorname{se}(\mathrm{pred})
=\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{
1+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(x_h-\bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}
}.
$$

$$
\hat{Y}_h\pm
t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(\mathrm{pred}).
$$

- Both intervals use the same center and $n-2$ degrees of freedom.
- The prediction interval adds irreducible individual-response variance
  $\hat{\sigma}^2$, represented by the extra 1 inside the square root.
- Therefore, the prediction interval is always wider.
- The mean-response standard error can approach zero as information increases.
- The prediction standard error cannot approach zero while $\hat{\sigma}>0$.


14. WHAT CONTROLS RESPONSE-INTERVAL WIDTH?

- Higher confidence or larger MSE makes the interval wider.
- Larger $n$ or larger $S_{xx}$ makes the interval narrower.
- Values of $x_h$ closer to $\bar{x}$ produce narrower intervals.
- Both intervals are narrowest at $x_h=\bar{x}$.
- Moving away from $\bar{x}$ increases uncertainty.
- Extrapolation beyond the observed predictor range is especially risky.

Classifying common questions:

| Question | Use |
|---|---|
| Average life expectancy at 2 packs/day | Mean-response confidence interval. |
| Lung function of one 80-year-old woman | Prediction interval. |
| Tomorrow's greenhouse humidity at 31 degrees C | Prediction interval. |
| Typical weight of women 65 inches tall | Mean-response confidence interval. |


15. REGRESSION ANOVA

Sum-of-squares and degrees-of-freedom decompositions:

$$
\mathrm{SSTO}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE},
\qquad
n-1=1+(n-2).
$$

| Source | df | SS | MS | F |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Regression | $1$ | SSR | $\mathrm{MSR}=\mathrm{SSR}/1=\mathrm{SSR}$ | $\mathrm{MSR}/\mathrm{MSE}$ |
| Error | $n-2$ | SSE | $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)$ | |
| Total | $n-1$ | SSTO | | |

Expected mean squares:

$$
E(\mathrm{MSE})=\sigma^2,
\qquad
E(\mathrm{MSR})=\sigma^2+\beta_1^2S_{xx}.
$$

If $\beta_1=0$, MSR and MSE both estimate $\sigma^2$, so their ratio should
be near 1. If $\beta_1\ne0$, MSR tends to exceed MSE.


16. F TEST FOR THE POPULATION SLOPE

$$
H_0:\beta_1=0
\qquad\text{vs}\qquad
H_1:\beta_1\ne0.
$$

$$
F^*=\frac{\mathrm{MSR}}{\mathrm{MSE}}
=\frac{\mathrm{SSR}/1}{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)}
\sim F(1,n-2)\quad\text{under }H_0.
$$

Reject for a sufficiently large $F^*$.

For simple linear regression:

$$
F^*=(t^*)^2.
$$

Therefore, the ANOVA F test and the two-sided slope t test have the same
p-value.

NOTE - Direction:
The F statistic is nonnegative and $\beta_1$ is squared in $E(\mathrm{MSR})$,
so this test is for $\beta_1\ne0$. Use the signed t statistic for
$\beta_1<0$ or $\beta_1>0$.


17. WORKED EXAMPLE: MORTALITY VS LATITUDE

Fitted line and sample size:

$$
\hat{Y}=389.1894-5.9776x,
\qquad
n=49.
$$

| Quantity | Value and interpretation |
|---|---|
| Slope | $-5.9776$ with SE $0.5984$. Each 1-degree increase in latitude is associated with about 5.98 fewer deaths per 10 million in mean mortality. |
| Slope test | $t=-9.9898$, $p=3.309\times10^{-13}$: very strong evidence that $\beta_1\ne0$. |
| Residual variation | $\hat{\sigma}=19.12$, MSE approximately $365$, error df $=47$. |
| Sums of squares | SSR $=36{,}464$, SSE $=17{,}173$, SSTO $=53{,}637$. |
| Goodness of fit | $R^2=0.6798$: 67.98% of mortality variation is explained by the fitted linear relationship with latitude. |
| ANOVA | $F=99.797$ on $(1,47)$ df; $F=t^2$. |
| At $x_h=40$ | $\hat{Y}_h=150.084$. Reported 95% prediction interval for one location: 111.235 to 188.933 deaths per 10 million. |

Arithmetic checks:
- $36{,}464+17{,}173=53{,}637$.
- $1+47=48$.
- $36{,}464/365\approx99.8$.
- $\mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SSTO}=0.6798$.
- The intercept predicts mortality at latitude 0, far outside the data range,
  so it is not substantively useful.


18. WORKED EXAMPLE: HEIGHT AND WEIGHT

$$
\hat{Y}=-266.53+6.14x,
\qquad
r=0.947,
\qquad
\mathrm{MSE}=74.673.
$$

- Each additional inch predicts about 6.14 lb higher mean weight.
- The intercept $-266.53$ lb is meaningless because $x=0$ inches is outside
  the data.
- Among proposed lines, the line with the smaller $\sum e_i^2$ is the better
  least-squares fit.


19. COMPLETE STATISTICAL CONCLUSION TEMPLATES

Slope test:
At $\alpha=$ [level], test $H_0:\beta_1=$ [value] versus
$H_1:$ [alternative]. The statistic is $t^*=$ [value] with $n-2$ df and
$p=$ [value]. Because $p$ [comparison] $\alpha$, [reject/fail to reject]
$H_0$. There [is/is not] sufficient evidence that [population statement].

Slope confidence interval:
We are $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confident that the population mean response changes
by between [L] and [U] response units for each one-unit increase in the
predictor.

Prediction interval:
We are $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confident that one new response at $x_h$ will lie
between [L] and [U] [response units].


20. COMPACT FORMULA SHEET

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Means | $\bar{x}=\sum x_i/n$, $\bar{Y}=\sum Y_i/n$ |
| Sums | $S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$, $S_{yy}=\sum(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2$, $S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(Y_i-\bar{Y})$ |

| Residual | $e_i=Y_i-\hat{Y}_i$, $\mathrm{SSE}=\sum e_i^2$ |
| Correlation | $r=S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$ |
| Variance | $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)$, $\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}$ |
| Coefficient SE | $\operatorname{se}(b_1)=\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$, $\operatorname{se}(b_0)=\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1/n+\bar{x}^2/S_{xx}}$ |
| R-squared | $R^2=\mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SSTO}=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SSTO}=r^2$ |
| Coefficient CI | $b_j\pm t_{n-2,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(b_j)$ |
| Coefficient test | $t^*=(b_j-\beta_{j0})/\operatorname{se}(b_j)$ |
| Mean-response CI | $\hat{Y}_h\pm t^*\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1/n+(x_h-\bar{x})^2/S_{xx}}$ |
| Prediction interval | $\hat{Y}_h\pm t^*\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1+1/n+(x_h-\bar{x})^2/S_{xx}}$ |
| ANOVA F | $F^*=\mathrm{MSR}/\mathrm{MSE}\sim F(1,n-2)$ under $H_0:\beta_1=0$; $F^*=(t^*)^2$ |


21. PROCEDURE DECISION GUIDE

| Question asks about | Use |
|---|---|
| Best-fitting line | Least squares: $b_0,b_1$. |
| Direction or strength | Scatterplot, $r$, and $R^2$. |
| Population slope or intercept | t interval or t test, df $=n-2$. |
| Average response at $x_h$ | Confidence interval for $E(Y_h)$. |
| One future outcome at $x_h$ | Prediction interval for $Y_{h(\mathrm{new})}$. |
| Regression ANOVA | SSR, SSE, SSTO, and $F=\mathrm{MSR}/\mathrm{MSE}$. |


22. COMMON EXAM TRAPS

- Using $n-1$ for MSE. Regression estimates two coefficients, so error
  df $=n-2$.
- Using MSE where a standard error requires
  $\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}$.
- Reporting $r=\sqrt{R^2}$ without the slope sign. Use
  $r=\operatorname{sign}(b_1)\sqrt{R^2}$.
- Saying the slope changes an individual response. It estimates change in the
  mean response.
- Using a prediction interval for an average, or a confidence interval for one
  future unit.
- Interpreting the intercept without checking whether $x=0$ is meaningful and
  within the observed range.
- Treating the p-value as $P(H_0\text{ is true})$.
- Writing "accept $H_0$" when $p\ge\alpha$.
- Claiming causation from observational association.
- Calling a high $R^2$ proof of a good linear model.
- Using F for a directional slope test; F loses the slope sign.


23. FINAL ANSWER CHECKLIST

- Define the population parameter and target population.
- State $H_0$, $H_1$, $\alpha$, statistic, degrees of freedom, and p-value
  when testing.
- Show the formula substitution or calculation, not just a final number.
- Name the correct target: coefficient, mean response, or one new response.
- Attach units to slopes, predicted values, and interval endpoints.
- Use "fail to reject," not "accept," and interpret the result in context.
- Check LINE conditions, influential points, and whether extrapolation is
  occurring.
- For $R^2$, state the percentage of response variation explained by the
  fitted linear model.


24. HIGH-YIELD TRUTHS

- The least-squares line passes through $(\bar{x},\bar{Y})$.
- Residuals sum to zero.
- Slope and correlation have the same sign; $R^2$ loses direction.
- Greater $S_{xx}$ improves slope precision.
- Response intervals are narrowest near $\bar{x}$.
- Prediction intervals are wider than mean-response confidence intervals.
- In simple regression, the slope t test and ANOVA F test are equivalent for
  the two-sided alternative.
- Statistical significance, practical importance, linear adequacy, and
  causality are different questions.
